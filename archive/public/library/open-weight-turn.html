Model economics · twelve-month read
Compiled 9 September 2026

The open-weight turn

Over the past year, freely downloadable models stopped being the cheap alternative and became the default. They now carry the majority of developer token traffic — while still collecting a sliver of the money. This is what changed, what it costs, and how much capability you actually get per token.

Usage: the crossover

Each band is the share of all tokens routed through OpenRouter going to open-weight labs versus closed labs. Open-weight means the model's weights can be downloaded; closed means you can only reach it through someone's API. The two always add to 100%.

Weekly averages. Solid line: compiled daily per-lab usage, 19 Mar – 8 Sep 2026. Dashed: anchored to OpenRouter's 100-trillion-token study, which put open-weight usage near 30% in late 2025.
01

The flip happened in early June 2026. On a seven-day average the lines crossed on 4 June; a few single days had already flipped in late May. By mid-June open-weight labs held 60%.

02

This was not a collapse of the closed labs. Their absolute volume kept climbing all year. Open-weight volume just grew faster on a marketplace that was itself tripling.

03

Since July the split has settled into a noisy band between 56% and 65%. It is no longer a trend line — it is an equilibrium with weekly swings.

04

The mechanism is price. A router sends work to whichever model clears the job cheapest, and that is exactly the buyer open-weight models were built for.

Everyone grew — the pie exploded

Total tokens routed per day, same source. Reading the share chart alone hides the more important fact: nothing here is a zero-sum fight yet. The whole market is expanding underneath both camps.

Trillions of tokens per day. Weekly averages, March–September 2026; the March figure is a single-day observation.
01

Traffic went from about 2.2 trillion tokens a day in mid-March to 12.2 trillion in early September — a 5.6× increase in under six months.

02

Most of that new volume is automated, not human. Coding agents, bulk classification and background pipelines: the work where a model that is 90% as good at 5% of the price wins every routing decision.

03

Average requests also got heavier — prompts roughly 4× longer and answers about 3× longer than a year earlier. Cheaper tokens did not reduce spending; they invited more of it.

Output per token: what you buy with a dollar

Capability is scored on a single 0–100 composite index. Price is a blended rate assuming four input tokens for every output token — roughly what an agent loop looks like. The higher and further left a model sits, the more capability it returns per token spent.

Open-weight Closed dashed = the efficient frontier · circle area = output speed
Horizontal axis is logarithmic. Blended price = (4 × input + 1 × output) ÷ 5. Index marked * is provisional in the source.
01

The frontier is brutally flat. Going from GLM-5.3 at $2.00 per million tokens to Claude Fable 5.1 at $18.00 buys 8 more index points — 9× the price for about 15% more capability.

02

Beyond roughly $3 per million tokens, the curve stops climbing. You are paying for reliability, context depth and instruction-following on hard tasks — not for raw benchmark score.

03

The best value on this chart is not a frontier lab. Qwen3.8-Flash scores 42 at $0.21 per million — one point more than a closed model costing six times as much.

04

But the index is a composite, not a referee. Ten points of index on a hard reasoning task are not the same as ten points on summarisation. Flat-looking curves still hide real gaps where it counts.

Cost to finish a real task

A better test than price-per-token: run the same demanding task end to end on each model and count what it actually costs, including the thinking tokens. This is where the cheap-token story gets complicated.

US dollars to complete one standardised agentic task at each model's strongest setting, as measured by Artificial Analysis. Number in brackets is that model's capability index.
01

GPT-6 Astra completes the task for $3.26 — barely half of what Claude Fable 5.1 charges for the same 53 index points. The two leaders are not priced alike at all.

02

DeepSeek V4-Flash finishes for 22 cents, about 1/15th of the frontier price, at 35 index points. For high-volume, low-stakes work that gap is decisive.

03

The honest reading: for the hardest 10% of tasks the premium tiers are still worth it. For the other 90%, the price difference is very hard to justify on output alone.

Two maps of the same industry

Tokens measure how much thinking gets done. Dollars measure who gets paid. In 2026 these have come almost completely apart, and most arguments about "who is winning" come from quoting one and meaning the other.

Left: share of tokens routed on an open developer platform, September 2026. Right: share of enterprise model spend. Different populations — read side by side, not as one number.
01

Open-weight models carry 56% of tokens but roughly 7% of enterprise money. The camp that lost the traffic race is still collecting nearly all the revenue.

02

There is a 17–20× price spread between the cheap open tier and frontier closed pricing. Equal tokens at that spread never produce equal revenue.

03

Neither map is the whole picture. A router over-represents cost-sensitive developers; an enterprise survey over-represents procurement contracts. Both are true.

What all those tokens are actually doing

Volume did not grow because more people started chatting. It grew because the unit of work changed: one request now carries far more input, thinks for longer, and produces far more output. Four things that each roughly tripled or quadrupled in a year.

Early 2025 against late 2025, from OpenRouter's 100-trillion-token study. Each pair is scaled independently — read the multiplier, not the bar heights across pairs.
01

Programming went from roughly a tenth of tokens to more than half. Most inference is now machines writing and reading code for other machines, not conversation.

02

Reasoning models went from negligible to over half of all tokens in under a year. Models that think before answering are now the default, and thinking tokens are billed like any other.

03

Requests got heavier in both directions — inputs roughly 4× longer, answers roughly 3× longer. Cheaper tokens did not cut spending; they invited longer work. That is why volume grew 5.6× while price per token fell.

Four other things worth knowing

The charts above answer "who" and "how much". These answer "how fast is it changing" and "what are the failure modes of reading these numbers".

The gap is not closing

Four months behind, all year

4 months · 8 points

Open-weight models have sat roughly four months behind the closed frontier since January 2026 — about the distance between one generation and the next of the same model family. They are not catching up; they are keeping pace at a discount.

Demand barely reacts to price

Cutting prices does not buy volume

0.5–0.7% per 10%

A 10% price cut lifts usage by only half a percent. People switch models because one is good enough, not because it got marginally cheaper. Capability and reliability decide; price only sets the floor.

Crowded at the bottom

Three labs carry the open side

92% from three labs

A year ago two models held over half of all open-weight traffic. Today Z.AI, DeepSeek and Xiaomi carry 92% of it between them — and the lead has already changed hands twice since June.

Cheap is getting less cheap

Open prices are moving up

3× increase

DeepSeek roughly tripled its list prices in August 2026 once demand locked in — input on the Flash tier went from about $0.15 to $0.44 per million. The open-weight discount is a strategy, not a law of nature, and it can be withdrawn.

12

What this means, plainly

01

Stop buying one model for everything. The spread between the cheapest measured model and a frontier flagship is now about 85× on price and 11 points on capability. Paying frontier rates for routine work is the single biggest waste in most AI budgets.

02

Route by difficulty, not by habit. Send classification, extraction, summarisation and first-draft generation to a sub-$1 model. Escalate to the frontier only when the cheap pass fails or the stakes are high.

03

Price your work per finished task, not per token. A model that costs 22 cents and needs three attempts is worse than one that costs two dollars and gets it right first time.

04

Don't build on a single open-weight family. No open model has held the lead for more than a few months this year. Keep your integration swappable.

05

Expect cheap tiers to reprice. The current discount is a land grab. Budget for the open-weight floor rising, because it already has once.

06

If you only watch the closed labs, you are watching the minority of inference. More than half of real traffic now runs on models most teams have never tested a prompt against.

Which model for which job

Everything above collapses into one operating rule: start at the cheapest tier that clears your task, and climb only when it fails. This is what each rung of that ladder currently looks like.

Bulk
$0.21 – $0.62 / M
Routing, tagging, extraction, classification, first drafts

High-volume work where any single failure is cheap and invisible. This is where most tokens actually go, and where the frontier tier is pure waste.

Qwen3.8-Flash · MiniMax-M3 · DeepSeek V4-Flash
Watch for: weak instruction-following on long prompts, and retries that quietly erase the price advantage. Measure accepted output, not calls made.
Everyday
$1.35 – $2.80 / M
Summarisation, RAG answers, routine code edits, support drafting

The default tier for production. Capability here is within a few index points of the frontier on most real tasks at a tenth of the price.

Gemini 3.8 Flash · GLM-5.3 · DeepSeek V4-Pro · Grok 4.6
Watch for: licence terms, single-provider concentration, and quality that varies noticeably by language and domain.
Demanding
$5.40 – $9.00 / M
Multi-step reasoning, long agent runs, anything a customer will read

Where reliability starts to matter more than unit cost. The step up from the everyday tier buys fewer retries and less human review.

Kimi K3 · GPT-5.6 Sol · Claude Opus 5
Watch for: cost compounding in agent loops. Cache aggressively and cap the number of steps, or a single session can outrun a month of batch work.
Frontier
$18.00 / M
The hardest tenth — where a wrong answer is expensive

Reserve it. The measurable capability gain over the demanding tier is roughly 10%, for double the price; the real gain is on tasks that cheaper models simply fail.

GPT-6 Astra · Claude Fable 5.1

01

Use two tiers, not one. Almost every workload that looks like it needs the frontier tier actually needs a cheap pass plus an escalation path for the failures.

02

Judge by finished task, not token price. The ranking changes once failures and retries are priced in — and inverts entirely if the cheap model cannot pass your acceptance check.

03

Re-run this ladder every quarter. Every rung has turned over at least twice in the last year.

How to read these numbers

  • Usage figures are one router, not the whole market
  • Token share is not revenue share
  • Open-weight here means downloadable weights, not a specific licence
  • List prices exclude caching, batch and enterprise discounts
  • Composite indices treat capability as linear, which it is not
  • Model generations move fast — this is a snapshot, not a standing verdict

What these numbers cannot tell you

Traffic is measured on one developer router. The largest closed labs sell most of their volume through their own APIs and consumer apps, none of which passes through it — so the platform structurally undercounts closed-model usage globally. A 56% open-weight share on this router is not a 56% share of all AI inference.

Enterprise spend figures come from a survey of buying decisions, not audited revenue, and open-weight usage inside companies running their own GPUs barely registers anywhere. The true open-weight share of dollars is a guess with wide error bars; treat 7% as an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

Capability indices compress a messy reality into one number. Two models with the same score can behave very differently on your specific workload. The only result that matters is the one measured on your own tasks.
